Fundamentals and definitions of formability

What "formability" actually means, and why it can never be reduced to a single material property.

Formability is the capacity of a metallic sheet to change shape by plastic deformation without failing — by localized necking, fracture, wrinkling, or springback outside tolerance — under the stress state, strain path, and boundary conditions imposed by a specific forming operation. It is a system property, not a material constant: the same coil of steel can be highly formable in a deep-drawn cup and marginal in a stretch-formed panel, because the two operations impose different stress states on the sheet.

The three families of sheet forming

Stretching

Sheet clamped at the edges (blank-holder locked); a punch stretches it biaxially. Thickness thins everywhere; limited by localized necking. Governs fender panels, Erichsen/Nakajima tests.

Drawing

Flange material flows radially inward into a die cavity under punch load; deformation is dominated by in-plane compression/tension, not thinning. Limited by wall fracture or flange wrinkling. Governs cups, Swift test.

Bending

Through-thickness strain gradient: tension outside the neutral axis, compression inside. Limited by outer-fiber fracture or springback. Governs flanges, hems, roll forming.

Real stamped parts combine all three in different regions of the same panel — which is exactly why a single index number (like an Erichsen value) can never fully rank formability across applications, and why the discipline needs both mechanistic plasticity models (Sections 5–10) and process-representative tests (Sections 3–4).

What limits formability

  • Diffuse necking — a long-wavelength thinning band that begins once the material's strain-hardening rate can no longer compensate for the loss of load-carrying cross-section (loss of the Considère condition). This is a geometric instability, not a fracture criterion.
  • Localized necking — a narrow, near through-thickness shear band that follows diffuse necking under plane-strain and stretching states; this is what the FLC actually marks (Section 4).
  • Ductile fracture — void nucleation, growth and coalescence at inclusions/second-phase particles, which can occur before or after necking depending on stress triaxiality and the strain path.
  • Wrinkling — a compressive plastic buckling instability in regions of low through-thickness/lateral support (flanges, unsupported panel areas).
  • Springback — elastic recovery after tool release; not a "failure" in the necking sense, but a formability limit for dimensional tolerance, and the reason Section 10's kinematic hardening models exist.
Measured in the lab

Local necking onset is measured via digital image correlation (DIC) on a speckled or electro-etched grid: full-field major/minor strain maps are computed from image pairs, and the strain history of the critical element is tracked up to the frame before the strain-rate in that element diverges from its neighbors — this divergence is the operational definition of "onset of localized necking" used to build FLCs (ISO 12004-2, Section 4).

Relationships between properties, microstructure, and forming capacity

Formability is a function of five coupled variables. Change one and the forming window moves.

Five factors jointly set the plastic forming capacity of a sheet, and none of them can be tuned in isolation — an alloying or texture change made to raise strength almost always moves at least one of the other four.

Strain hardening and formability

The strain-hardening exponent n (Section 8) is the single mechanical parameter most correlated with stretch formability, because it governs how long uniform (diffuse) straining can continue before necking under the Considère criterion:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon} = \sigma \quad\Rightarrow\quad \varepsilon_{u} = n \ \text{(for a Hollomon solid under uniaxial tension)}$$
εu
true strain at maximum load (onset of diffuse necking, uniaxial tension)
n
strain-hardening exponent from a Hollomon fit σ = Kεn

A higher n spreads strain more uniformly before instability, which is why stretch-critical panels favor high-n grades (e.g., interstitial-free or TRIP steels), while deep-drawn parts favor a high normal anisotropy r̄ instead — the two requirements do not always point to the same microstructure.

Measured in the lab

Lankford coefficients are obtained from uniaxial tensile specimens cut at 0°, 45°, and 90° to the rolling direction: r = εw/εt, the ratio of true width strain to true thickness strain, measured between roughly 10% and 15–20% elongation (before necking corrupts the width/thickness measurement). Thickness strain is usually back-calculated from volume constancy (εt = −εl − εw) rather than measured directly, since sheet thickness gauges are far less precise than width extensometry.

03 / 12

Technological sheet metal formability tests

Five standardized tests, five different strain paths — none of them equivalent, all of them necessary.

"Technological" tests deliberately reproduce a forming stress state rather than the pure uniaxial state of a tensile test. Each imposes a different strain path, so each ranks alloys differently — a sheet can top the Erichsen ranking and sit mid-pack on the Swift ranking.

Erichsen / Olsen

Hemispherical punch (Ø20 mm) drives into a fully clamped blank until through-crack. Reports the Erichsen Index (IE), the punch depth (mm) at fracture — pure biaxial stretch, near-frictionless, ISO 20482.

Swift cup

Flat-bottom cylindrical punch draws a circular blank into a die. Blank diameter is increased until the cup wall fractures; reports the Limiting Drawing Ratio, LDR = Dmax/dpunch — dominated by radial drawing, not stretching.

Fukui conical cup

A conical punch forms a blank with no blank-holder; combines drawing and stretching. Reports the conical cup value; less common today but historically used to separate drawability from stretchability in one setup.

Nakajima

Hemispherical punch over specimens of varying width — from a full circle (equibiaxial) to a narrow strip (near-uniaxial), passing through plane strain. Generates the family of strain paths used to trace the FLC (ISO 12004-2).

Marciniak

A flat-bottom punch deforms the test blank through a perforated carrier sheet, which removes punch friction and bending from the gauge region beneath the punch — giving a more rigorous, geometry-independent plane-strain-to-biaxial branch of the FLC than Nakajima.

None of these five tests is normally reproduced directly inside a stamping FE model. Instead, their outputs calibrate or validate the model: Nakajima/Marciniak strain pairs build the FLC used as a post-processing forming-severity criterion overlaid on simulated strain states (Section 4), while Erichsen/Swift results are typically used only as go/no-go acceptance checks against simulation-predicted thinning and drawability margins.

04 / 12

Forming limit curves and diagrams (FLC/FLD)

The single most-used formability criterion in industry — and one of the most misused, because it is path-dependent.

The Forming Limit Diagram (FLD) plots major true surface strain ε1 (vertical) against minor true surface strain ε2 (horizontal) measured at the onset of localized necking, for specimens deformed along many different strain paths (produced by the Nakajima/Marciniak specimen family, Section 3). The curve connecting these limit points is the Forming Limit Curve (FLC): strain states below it are considered formable, at or above it are at risk of splitting.

Determining the FLC (ISO 12004-2)

  1. Deform a set of specimens of graded width (equibiaxial → uniaxial) until fracture, recording full-field strain history by DIC.
  2. For each specimen, identify the section through the crack/neck and extract the strain-versus-time (or strain-versus-position) profile in the last frames before fracture.
  3. Apply the ISO cross-section / time-dependent method: fit strain-rate profiles on either side of the forming neck's inflection points and extrapolate back to the frame where the neck's strain-rate first diverges from its neighbors — this frame's (ε1,ε2) pair is the limit strain, deliberately excluding the post-necking frames where strain is no longer representative of the sheet at large.
  4. Repeat across the specimen set and fit a curve (often a second-order polynomial or piecewise-linear form) through the resulting limit points.
Measured in the lab

A useful, purely empirical estimate of the plane-strain intercept FLC0 for low-carbon steels (Keeler–Brazier):

$$FLC_0=\left(23.3+14.13\,t\right)\frac{n}{0.21}\quad[\%]$$

Interpretation and the path-dependence caveat

The left branch (ε2<0, drawing side) has an approximate slope of −1, reflecting near-constant thickness strain regardless of the ε1/ε2 ratio; the right branch (ε2>0, stretching side) rises less steeply because equibiaxial stretching adds thinning contributions from two in-plane directions at once. The minimum, at ε2≈0 (plane strain), is the most restrictive point on the curve — which is exactly why so much die-design effort goes into avoiding plane-strain states over sharp radii and character lines.

The classical FLC assumes a proportional (linear) strain path from the undeformed state. Real stampings often load non-proportionally — e.g., a pre-draw stage followed by a stretch stage — and the same final (ε1,ε2) point can be safe or unsafe depending on the path taken to reach it. This is the main motivation for the Forming Limit Stress Diagram (FLSD): converting the strain-based limit to principal-stress space using the flow curve and yield function makes the limit far less path-sensitive, at the cost of needing an accurate hardening + yield model (Sections 6–9) to do the conversion.

Used in simulation

In FE post-processing, every integration point's simulated (ε1,ε2) trajectory is overlaid on the FLC, usually with a safety margin band (e.g., 10% below the curve = marginal). Because the FLC is path-dependent, codes that see strongly non-proportional loading increasingly report results against an FLSD-derived criterion or a ductile-fracture criterion (Section 11) instead of the strain-based FLC alone.

Fundamentals of plasticity applied to metal forming

Reducing a general 3-D stress state to a single scalar the material "feels" — and the flow rule that turns it back into a strain increment.

Metal forming stresses are almost never uniaxial. Plasticity theory needs a scalar measure that lets a multiaxial stress state be compared against the single-axis flow curve measured in a tensile test. That scalar is the equivalent (von Mises, unless stated) stress, and its work-conjugate is the equivalent plastic strain.

$$\bar\sigma=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2+(\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2+(\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2\right]}\qquad d\bar\varepsilon^{p}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}\,d\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}\,d\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}}$$
σ1,2,3
principal stresses
σ̄
equivalent (von Mises) stress — compared directly against the flow curve σ̄=σY(ε̄p)
dε̄p
equivalent plastic strain increment, integrated over the loading history to give ε̄p

Yield function, yield surface, and the flow rule

A yield function f(σij,ε̄p) = σ̄(σij) − σY(ε̄p) defines a closed surface in stress space, the yield surface: f<0 is elastic, f=0 is yielding, f>0 is inadmissible. Plastic strain increments are assumed to follow the normality (associated flow) rule — they point along the outward normal to the yield surface at the current stress point:

$$d\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}=d\lambda\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma_{ij}}$$
dλ
plastic multiplier (≥0), fixed by the consistency condition df=0 during plastic loading
∂f/∂σij
gradient of the yield function — the flow direction; this is why the shape of the yield surface (Sections 6–7), not just its size, controls how strain partitions between directions
Used in simulation

Every explicit or implicit FE step that returns a trial elastic stress outside the yield surface performs a return-mapping (radial return, for von Mises with isotropic hardening) to project it back onto the updated surface, consuming Δε̄p in the process. The choice of yield function in Sections 6–7 changes the direction of that projection, which is why swapping von Mises for Hill'48 in a material card changes predicted thinning distributions even when the flow curve is identical.

Isotropic yield criteria: Tresca and von Mises

The two textbook criteria — and why real sheet almost never obeys either one exactly.

$$\text{Tresca:}\quad \bar\sigma_T=\max\big(|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|,\,|\sigma_2-\sigma_3|,\,|\sigma_3-\sigma_1|\big)$$ $$\text{von Mises:}\quad \bar\sigma_{vM}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2+(\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2+(\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2\right]}$$

Tresca assumes yielding is controlled purely by the maximum shear stress; von Mises assumes it is controlled by the distortional (shape-change) elastic strain energy, equivalently the octahedral shear stress. In plane stress, Tresca's locus is a hexagon inscribed inside the von Mises ellipse, coinciding at uniaxial and equibiaxial points and diverging most (≈15%) at pure shear.

Interactive · Isotropic yield lociPlane stress, σ₁–σ₂ plane, normalized by σY

Both loci pass through the same uniaxial (1,0), (0,1) and equibiaxial (1,1) points — the criteria only disagree about the path connecting them, which is exactly the shear and near-plane-strain region most relevant to bending and flanging.

Max discrepancy: ≈15.5%
at pure shear (σ₁=−σ₂)
Used in simulation

Von Mises is the default isotropic option in essentially every commercial FE code because its flow rule is smooth everywhere (no corners), giving a unique, well-conditioned return-mapping direction; Tresca's hexagonal corners require special corner-return logic and are rarely used for sheet forming in practice, mainly retained for pressure-vessel/structural codes with conservative design margins.

Anisotropic plasticity: Hill '48 and the Barlat family

Rolled sheet is not isotropic. Its yield surface must encode that, or predicted strain distributions and earing profiles will be wrong.

Hill's 1948 quadratic criterion

Hill's model generalizes von Mises with six anisotropy coefficients (F,G,H,L,M,N) referred to the material's orthotropic axes (rolling, transverse, normal). For a sheet loaded with principal stresses aligned to the rolling/transverse directions (no in-plane shear), it reduces to a function of the Lankford coefficients measured at 0°, 45°, and 90° to rolling:

$$G\,\sigma_1^{2}+F\,\sigma_2^{2}+H(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^{2}=\bar\sigma_0^{2}\,,\qquad G=\frac{1}{1+r_0},\quad H=\frac{r_0}{1+r_0},\quad F=\frac{r_0}{r_{90}(1+r_0)}$$
σ1, σ2
in-plane principal stresses along rolling and transverse directions
r0, r45, r90
Lankford coefficients at 0°, 45°, 90° to rolling (r45 enters the shear coefficient N, not shown here since no shear stress is present in this reduced principal form)
σ̄0
reference (rolling-direction) uniaxial yield stress

Hill'48 is cheap to calibrate (three tensile tests give r0, r45, r90) and remains the default anisotropic option in most commercial codes, but it is known to give qualitatively wrong yield-locus curvature for many aluminum alloys and some high-strength steels, particularly under-predicting the balanced-biaxial yield stress.

The Barlat family (YLD89, YLD91, YLD2000-2d, YLD2004-18p)

Barlat's criteria replace Hill's quadratic form with a higher-order, non-quadratic function of linearly transformed stresses — the Cauchy stress tensor is first passed through one or two fourth-order linear transformation tensors (whose components are the anisotropy coefficients) before being combined in a Hosford-type exponent form. This decouples "shape flexibility" (via the exponent m) from "directionality" (via the transformation coefficients), letting the same family fit both the yield-stress and r-value directionality far more accurately than Hill'48 — at the cost of needing more tests to calibrate (typically 0/45/90° tensile yield stresses and r-values, plus a balanced-biaxial test) and non-linear fitting.

$$\text{Isotropic exponent form (Hosford), the conceptual seed of the Barlat family:}$$ $$|\sigma_1|^{m}+|\sigma_2|^{m}+|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|^{m}=2\bar\sigma_0^{m}$$
m
exponent; m=2 recovers von Mises exactly, m→∞ recovers Tresca; m≈6 fits BCC metals (steels), m≈8 fits FCC metals (aluminum) best (Logan & Hosford)

Barlat's YLD2000-2d applies this exponent form to two linearly transformed stress tensors instead of the raw principal stresses, each transformation carrying its own set of anisotropy coefficients (α1…α8) fitted to the measured directional yield stresses and r-values — the widget below uses the untransformed Hosford exponent form as a stand-in to show what the exponent alone does to locus shape; production Barlat calibration additionally warps the locus directionally, which a 1-parameter demo cannot show.

Measured in the lab

A full Barlat YLD2000-2d calibration typically needs: uniaxial yield stress and r-value at 0°, 45°, 90° to rolling (six numbers, from three tensile tests with extensometry in two directions each), plus the balanced-biaxial yield stress and biaxial r-value from a hydraulic bulge test or a cruciform biaxial rig. Hill'48 needs only the three r-values (yield stresses are often assumed isotropic to first order).

Used in simulation

The yield function is entered in the FE material card as a coefficient set (Hill'48: F,G,H,N or equivalently r-values; Barlat: α1…α8 and m), and every integration point evaluates it once per iteration to get both σ̄ (for the hardening comparison) and ∂f/∂σ (for the flow direction) — so an error in these coefficients biases both the predicted force/thinning level and the predicted strain-path direction, which then feeds directly into the FLC check of Section 4.

Strain hardening modeling: Hollomon, Ludwik, Swift, Voce

Four curve shapes, four different assumptions about how far hardening can run — and very different extrapolation risk past the necking strain.

$$\text{Hollomon:}\ \sigma=K\varepsilon^{n}\qquad \text{Ludwik:}\ \sigma=\sigma_0+K\varepsilon^{n}\qquad \text{Swift:}\ \sigma=K(\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon)^{n}\qquad \text{Voce:}\ \sigma=\sigma_0+Q\!\left(1-e^{-b\varepsilon}\right)$$
σ, ε
true (flow) stress and true plastic strain
K
strength coefficient (Hollomon/Ludwik/Swift)
n
strain-hardening exponent — Hollomon forces σ→0 as ε→0, which is unphysical at very small strain but fits well over the technologically relevant range
σ0
initial/back-extrapolated yield stress (Ludwik, Voce)
ε0
Swift's pre-strain offset — shifts the origin so σ≠0 at ε=0 without a separate additive term
Q, b
Voce saturation stress increment and rate — Voce is the only one of the four with a finite hardening ceiling (σ→σ0+Q as ε→∞), matching metals that saturate (many aluminum alloys, austenitic stainless) rather than power-law metals (many steels)
Interactive · Hardening law comparisonσ vs. true plastic strain ε
Measured in the lab

Coefficients are fit to the true stress–true plastic strain curve from a uniaxial tensile test, valid only up to the diffuse-necking strain (Section 1) where the stress state stops being uniaxial. Beyond that strain, the flow curve must be extrapolated (the four laws above disagree increasingly as ε grows) or extended experimentally with a hydraulic bulge test, which reaches true strains 2–4× higher than tension under a near-equibiaxial stress state and is converted to an equivalent flow curve via the membrane (thin-shell) equations and a chosen yield function (Section 7).

Used in simulation

The hardening law sets σY(ε̄p) in the yield function f=σ̄−σY(ε̄p) (Section 5); because stamping simulations routinely reach local strains well beyond the tensile necking limit, the choice of extrapolation law materially changes predicted punch force and thinning in high-strain regions — a common practical compromise is a combined Swift–Voce law, blending Swift's power-law rise at low strain with Voce's saturation at high strain via a weighting parameter fit to bulge-test data.

Isotropic, kinematic, and combined hardening

Hardening does more than one thing: it can grow the yield surface, shift it, or both — and only one of those choices predicts the Bauschinger effect.

ModelYield surface evolutionReverse-loading prediction
IsotropicSurface expands uniformly about a fixed center; f = σ̄(σ) − R(ε̄p) − σY0Predicts reverse yield at the same magnitude as forward flow stress — no Bauschinger effect. Adequate for monotonic, proportional forming only.
KinematicSurface translates rigidly by a back-stress α, size constant; f = σ̄(σ−α) − σY0Predicts early reverse yielding (Bauschinger effect) but no additional hardening capacity — flow stress magnitude on reload is capped.
CombinedBoth expand (R) and translate (α); f = σ̄(σ−α) − R(ε̄p) − σY0Captures both early re-yield and continued hardening — required for any process with load reversal (Section 10).
$$\text{Nonlinear (Armstrong–Frederick) kinematic hardening:}\qquad d\alpha = C\,d\varepsilon^{p} - \gamma\,\alpha\,d\bar\varepsilon^{p}$$
α
back-stress tensor (1-D form shown), locates the current center of the yield surface
C
initial (linear) kinematic hardening modulus — controls the slope right after reversal
γ
dynamic-recovery coefficient — controls how quickly α saturates to αsat=C/γ, which is what makes the model nonlinear and finite-range instead of linear and unbounded (Prager's original 1955 model set γ=0)
Used in simulation

Combined hardening is the standard choice for springback prediction, because a stamped panel unloads elastically then often re-loads plastically in the opposite sense during trim, flange, or in-service loading — a pure isotropic model will over-predict the springback-driving stress gradient through the thickness by missing the reduced reverse flow stress the material actually exhibits.

Bauschinger effect and the Yoshida–Uemori model

Why a two-surface model, not just a nonlinear back-stress, is needed to reproduce what a real cyclic sheet test shows.

The Bauschinger effect is the reduction in yield stress on load reversal following forward plastic straining — a direct consequence of directional dislocation structures (pile-ups, cell walls) built up during forward flow, which assist dislocation motion in the reverse direction. It is not captured by isotropic hardening at all, and only partially captured by a single nonlinear kinematic term: real sheet also shows a transient, rapidly decaying reverse hardening rate and, after several cycles, a workhardening stagnation — a near-flat region on the flow curve just after reversal — that a single Armstrong–Frederick back-stress cannot reproduce simultaneously with the correct saturation behavior.

The Yoshida–Uemori (Y-U) model resolves this with two nested surfaces: a small yield surface (radius Y) that moves inside a larger bounding surface (radius B), both translating via their own back-stresses. The bounding surface's motion carries the "true" hardening; the yield surface's relative position inside it reproduces the sharp, decaying early-reverse transient (Bauschinger) while an added workhardening-stagnation term captures the flat region — making Y-U the standard choice in commercial stamping/springback codes (e.g., as a user or built-in material model in LS-DYNA, PAM-STAMP, AutoForm) whenever multi-stage or cyclic-bending processes are being simulated.

Measured in the lab

Y-U/kinematic-hardening parameters are calibrated from a tension–compression test on a sheet specimen (needing anti-buckling fixtures/guides because sheet is thin), or from a cyclic bending–unbending test — both reverse the load path at controlled strain amplitudes and record the resulting hysteresis loops, which are then fit to extract C, γ (and the Y-U-specific bounding-surface and stagnation parameters) by least-squares matching of the simulated to the measured loops.

Applying plasticity models to numerical simulation of forming

How the pieces from Sections 5–10 actually sit inside a finite-element material subroutine.

A sheet-forming FE simulation (explicit dynamic, for the fast, highly nonlinear contact of stamping; implicit static, for slow processes or springback) needs, at every integration point and every time step, a way to update stress given an increment of strain. The material model (constitutive subroutine) that does this bundles exactly the four ingredients built up in this module:

  1. Elastic law — usually isotropic Hooke's law, used for the trial elastic stress predictor and for unloading/springback.
  2. Yield function (Sections 6–7) — evaluated on the trial stress to test f≤0; if violated, triggers plastic correction.
  3. Hardening law (Sections 8–9) — supplies σY(ε̄p) (and, if kinematic, α) needed by the yield function.
  4. Flow rule (Section 5) — gives the direction of the plastic correction (return mapping) once the yield function confirms plastic loading.
$$\text{Return mapping (implicit, radial-return form for von Mises + isotropic hardening):}$$ $$\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_{n+1}^{trial}-2G\,\Delta\bar\varepsilon^{p}\,\frac{3}{2}\frac{s_{n+1}^{trial}}{\bar\sigma^{trial}},\qquad \text{solve } \bar\sigma^{trial}-3G\Delta\bar\varepsilon^{p}=\sigma_Y(\bar\varepsilon^{p}_n+\Delta\bar\varepsilon^{p})$$
σn+1trial
elastic-predictor stress, computed assuming the whole strain increment is elastic
G
elastic shear modulus
strial
deviatoric part of the trial stress
Δε̄p
unknown plastic strain increment, solved by a local Newton iteration on the scalar consistency equation shown — this is the one nonlinear equation that must be solved per integration point, per time step, which is why efficient return-mapping algorithms matter enormously for run time on million-element stamping meshes

Non-quadratic (Barlat) and anisotropic (Hill) yield functions replace the simple radial-return direction with a locus-specific gradient ∂f/∂σ, generally requiring a full local Newton solve on the stress tensor rather than the scalar shortcut above; combined hardening adds α as a second evolving internal variable solved simultaneously with Δε̄p.

Used in simulation

Explicit codes (LS-DYNA, PAM-STAMP, Radioss) typically implement these as compiled material models selected by a numeric ID (e.g., LS-DYNA MAT_36/MAT_133 for Barlat variants, MAT_125 for combined hardening) or as user-supplied subroutines (UMAT for Abaqus/Standard implicit, VUMAT for Abaqus/Explicit) when a research yield function like Y-U or a new Barlat variant isn't natively available — writing one requires implementing exactly the four ingredients above plus their consistent (algorithmic) tangent stiffness for implicit convergence.

Measured in the lab

Validation grid strain is measured the same way as FLC calibration (Section 4): an electro-etched or printed circle grid on the blank, imaged before and after forming (or continuously by stereo-DIC during the press stroke), giving a full-field strain map to compare pointwise against simulation output — not just a single "did it crack" pass/fail, since a simulation can predict the right crack location for the wrong strain-path reason.

Used in simulation

A mismatch at validation almost always traces back to one of exactly four places: the flow curve extrapolation (Section 8) if thinning is wrong everywhere; the yield function (Sections 6–7) if the strain-path direction is wrong but magnitude is close; the hardening type (Section 9) if springback or a multi-stage stage-2 force is wrong; or friction/contact modeling if only the flange/draw-in region disagrees — plasticity model errors and tribology errors are the two most commonly conflated causes of a bad correlation, and separating them is usually the first diagnostic step.
